How to read this chapter

Ifá isn't something you can calculate. It is a divination system. A babaláwo (an Ifá priest) casts a sign with sixteen palm nuts (ìkín) or a divining chain (ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀), with the client present, and then recites verses. Nobody can work out a person's real birth Odù from a date. So this chapter does two separate things, and I keep them apart throughout:

  1. Everything the calendar and sky really give. These are the weekday, the Yoruba four-day week, the Kọ́jọ́dá date, the Akan soul-day name, the Akan 42-day cycle, the Dogon Sigui position, and the sky over Johnstown at your birth. These items are actually computed.
  2. A constructed cast. This is a fixed, repeatable stand-in for the cast a babaláwo would make. It comes from the SHA-256 hash of the string 1998-10-13T21:25Z|40.33N|78.92W|M. The first eight bits give the two legs of the chain, and the later bits give the ìbò, the cowrie cast and the kola cast. Every result from it is labelled CONSTRUCTED. It shows how the system works using your own data. It does not replace a real itá.

The full checklist of items is in deep/27_ifa_inventory.md. The code is in deep/27/compute.py and the results are in deep/27/result.json.

Part I — The Odù

1. The sixteen principal Odù

Ifá has sixteen "mother" signs, called Ojú Odù or Méjì. In the usual seniority order they are Ogbè, Ọ̀yẹ̀kú, Ìwòrì, Òdí, Ìrosùn, Ọ̀wọ́nrín, Ọ̀bàrà, Ọ̀kànràn, Ògúndá, Ọ̀sá, Ìká, Òtúúrúpọ̀n, Òtúrá, Ìrẹtẹ̀, Ọ̀ṣẹ́ and Òfún (Abimbọ́la, Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus, 1976; Bascom, Ifa Divination, 1969). Each one is a column of four marks. A single stroke (I) is one mark and a double stroke (II) is two. Ogbè is all single, light and open. Ọ̀yẹ̀kú is all double, night and closed.

What this means for you: both halves of your constructed sign come from the first five, the most senior group. Ọ̀yẹ̀kú is second in rank and Ìrosùn is fifth. In Ifá thinking, seniority means weight and age, not luck. Your sign is made of old material.

2. The 256 Odù

When two different principal Odù appear on the right and left legs, the result is one of 240 "child" signs (Ọmọ Odù or Àmúlù). Together with the 16 Méjì, that makes 16 × 16 = 256. The sign is named right leg first. Each of the 256 has its own body of ẹsẹ̀ (verses) (Abimbọ́la 1976; Bascom 1969). The cell number in the figure is just a row-by-row index. The seniority order of the 240 differs from lineage to lineage.

What this means for you: your constructed sign is Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Ìrosùn (cell 21). It is a child sign, not a Méjì. A child sign is read as the right Odù acting through the left one. So for you that is night, ancestors and endings, working through memory, lineage and red camwood.

3. The constructed cast

On a real ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀ chain, each leg has four half-seed pods. Concave side up gives one mark and convex up gives two (Bascom 1969). Here bits 1–4 of the hash gave 2 2 2 2 = Ọ̀yẹ̀kú on the right and bits 5–8 gave 1 1 2 2 = Ìrosùn on the left.

What this means for you: Ọ̀yẹ̀kú means the closing of the day, the ancestors, and what has to be honoured before anything new can start. Ìrosùn is tied to ọsùn (red camwood powder) and to remembering. In practice its verses often deal with the family line and with keeping a name alive. Put together, the sign describes someone who moves forward by honouring what came before, whose success is a kind of remembering.

4. Ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀ and ìkín: two ways to the same sign

Ìkín is the more senior method. The babaláwo holds the sixteen nuts and tries to grab them all in one hand. If one nut is left over he marks II. If two are left he marks I. Any other result doesn't count and he grabs again. He repeats this eight times. The ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀ gives all eight marks in a single throw (Bascom 1969). Both methods produce the same 256 signs.

What this means for you: the constructed cast imitates the ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀, the quick daily method. A birth or initiation reading would normally use ìkín. That is one more reason to treat the constructed sign as a sketch.

5. Ọpọ́n Ifá, the tray

The marks are drawn with a finger in ìyẹ̀rọ̀sùn, the powdered wood dust spread on the ọpọ́n. The carved face on the rim is Èṣù, the messenger who carries the sign between people and the gods (Abimbọ́la 1976). The image above shows Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Ìrosùn drawn into the powder with PIL.

What this means for you: the sign sits under Èṣù's eye. In Ifá no reading counts until Èṣù has carried it, which is why ẹbọ (offering) always includes his share.

6. Ìbò, the determinant

After the sign comes out, the babaláwo asks yes/no questions using paired objects held in the client's hands, such as a cowrie (yes) and a bone (no). The first question is whether the sign comes with ire (good fortune) or ibi (misfortune). Then he narrows down which kind of ire: long life, money, a spouse, children, ìṣẹ́gun (victory) and so on. The constructed ìbò came out ire, kind ire ìṣẹ́gun, victory over opposition.

What this means for you: ire ìṣẹ́gun is the "your road opens against resistance" answer. It doesn't promise there will be no opposition. It assumes there will be, and promises you come through.

7. Ẹsẹ̀ Ifá: the verses of Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Ìrosùn

Ifá verses follow a fixed pattern. A priest's name, a client's problem, the prescribed ẹbọ, the result, and a closing song. For this sign, published practitioner commentaries (for example the openifa / ifa-wisdom pages for Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Ìrosùn) stress the client's line and ancestors and patience before results. Their passages about bodily illness are left out here on purpose, because this book has a rule against health content. I don't quote any verse text that I couldn't check against a published corpus.

What this means for you: the recurring advice is to make your forebears part of your plans. That can be as simple as telling their story and carrying their name forward.

8. Èèwọ̀ (taboos) and ẹbọ (offerings)

Each Odù brings èèwọ̀ and an ẹbọ, but only the casting priest assigns them, based on the ìbò answers (Bascom 1969). None are invented here.

What this means for you: if you ever go for a real reading, the taboos you're given will come from that reading, not from this chapter.

9. Mẹ́rìndínlógún (sixteen cowries)

Priests of the òrìṣà, rather than babaláwo, throw sixteen cowries and count the number that land mouth up. Each count from 1 to 16 is an odù. Seven is Òdí (Bascom, Sixteen Cowries, 1980). The constructed throw gave 7.

What this means for you: in cowrie divination Òdí is a sign about something hidden or closed that has to be brought out properly. That fits with your Ifá halves, night and memory.

10. Obì (kola)

Four kola lobes are thrown. Three up and one down is Ìtagbà (Ẹtawa in some places), which means "yes, but confirm it." The constructed cast gave Ìtagbà. The names for each result vary by region (Bascom 1969).

What this means for you: this is a yes that asks for a second step. It agrees with ire ìṣẹ́gun: the result is good, but you have to follow it up.

Synthesis of Part I: all four constructed casts point the same way. Ifá gives Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Ìrosùn with ire ìṣẹ́gun. The cowries give Òdí, the hidden thing brought out. The kola gives a yes that needs confirming. The picture is of a person whose progress comes from honouring lineage and ancestors and from meeting resistance, and whose rewards come in a second movement rather than the first.

Part II — Birth customs

11. Ẹsẹ̀ntáyé (Àkọsẹ̀jáyé): the sign "of the feet"

On the third day after a birth, a babaláwo places the baby's feet on the ọpọ́n, or presents the child to Ifá, and casts the sign that the child "stepped into the world" with. That sign guides how the child is raised and named, and what taboos apply (jolan.com.ng; ondestinyspath.com 2018; see the practitioner essay at JSTOR 10.2307/1145850). Your ẹsẹ̀ntáyé was never cast. The constructed sign stands in for it.

What this means for you: if the constructed sign had been your real ẹsẹ̀ntáyé, a family following custom would have taught you your lineage history early on, because Ìrosùn's theme is memory.

12. The naming day

The naming ceremony (ìsọmọlórúkọ) traditionally takes place on the 7th day for girls and the 9th for boys, with the 8th for twins in many towns. Some names come straight from Ifá: Fálọ́lá, Ifáṣeun, Awóyẹmí. A name with "Ifá" or "Awo" in it can mark a child whose sign or birth day belongs to Ifá.

What this means for you: because you were born on the Ifá day (§16), a traditional family could have given you an Ifá-root name. In Yoruba terms your birthday already carries that.

13. Ìtẹ̀fá and the itá

Ìtẹ̀fá is initiation into Ifá. During it the initiate's personal Odù (Odù Ifá) is cast, and on the third day there is an itá, a full reading of that person's life (Abimbọ́la 1976; Maupoil 1943 for the Fon parallel). That is the only real "birth chart" in Ifá, and you can't compute it.

What this means for you: this is the main gap in the chapter, and it can only be filled by a living priest.

14. Odù Ọdún, the sign of the year

Every year many communities and temples cast an Odù for the coming year at the Ifá festival. I couldn't find a published Odù Ọdún for the year 1998–99 that I could check. This is stated as a gap.

15. Ilà (facial marks)

Ilà show which lineage and town a person belongs to. They don't come from the birth sign.

Portraits: no Odù verse, Fá text, Akan day-name lore or Dogon source describes the face of a person born under a particular sign or day. No tradition gives a face for you. The portrait-style artwork below is atmosphere only.

Synthesis of Part II: the customs around birth (the third-day reading, the naming day, the itá) are exactly the things that can't be done by calculation. What carries over is the pattern. A child's sign is meant to be read out loud and turned into a name and a set of rules. Your calendar items, especially the Ifá day, are the closest thing you have to that.

Part III — Calendar

16. The Yoruba four-day week (ọ̀sẹ̀)

The traditional Yoruba week has four days, each sacred to a deity. Under the Kọ́jọ́dá convention the order is Ọjọ́ Ọbàtálá, Ọjọ́ Ṣàngó (Jàkúta), Ọjọ́ Ọ̀rúnmìlà (Ọjọ́ Awo, Ifá's day) and Ọjọ́ Ògún. Markets in many towns run on this cycle (Wikipedia "Yoruba calendar"; NACAPA Kọ́jọ́dá; ipilese.com). Counting from the published anchor (1 June 2011 = Ọjọ́ Ọbàtálá), 13 October 1998 falls on Ọjọ́ Ọ̀rúnmìlà, the day of Ifá itself. The Ìpìlẹ̀ṣẹ̀ texts point out that the day order and starting point vary from town to town, so this is the result under the Kọ́jọ́dá convention.

What this means for you: this is the strongest calendar item in the chapter. You were born on the day set aside for Ọ̀rúnmìlà, the witness of destiny, the same god whose system this chapter is about. In Yoruba terms that's a birth inside the house of wisdom and divination.

17. The Kọ́jọ́dá date

Kọ́jọ́dá is a modern standardised version of the calendar. The year starts on 3 June, the count begins at 1958 = 10000, and each month has 28 days (seven four-day weeks). Under it your birth is year 10040, month 5, day 21. The Gregorian-aligned name for October is Ọ̀wàrà, the month of heavy rains and storms (Wikipedia; NACAPA). The Ìpìlẹ̀ṣẹ̀ material warns that the fixed new year and the 10,000-year count are 20th-century reconstructions.

What this means for you: day 21 of a 28-day month opens the last week of the month. You arrived in the closing stretch, which echoes Ọ̀yẹ̀kú's theme of endings. Ọ̀wàrà, the storm month, matches ire ìṣẹ́gun's victory through turbulence.

18. The Moon (Òṣùpá) at birth

The Moon was a waning crescent, 35% lit, below the Johnstown horizon. The previous new moon was 20 September and the next full moon 4 November 1998 (ephem). Yoruba months were traditionally counted from one new crescent to the next (Wikipedia).

What this means for you: you were born in the dark, closing half of the moon's month, another Ọ̀yẹ̀kú-coloured signal.

19. Market cycles

Because markets follow the four-day week, a day sacred to Ọ̀rúnmìlà is also a particular market day in towns that keep this cycle.

What this means for you: traditionally your birth day would have been a day of exchange and consultation, with people seeing a diviner before trading.

Synthesis of Part III: the calendar gives you the Ifá day, a late-month date in the storm month, and a waning moon. All three lean the same way as the constructed sign: wisdom, closing and inheritance, and coming through turbulence.

Part IV — Orí and destiny

20. Orí inú and Orí òde

Orí is the "inner head", your personal divinity of destiny. Orí òde is the physical head. Ifá teaches that no òrìṣà can help a person their Orí does not support (Abimbọ́la 1976; Akanmidu, PhilArchive AKATIA).

What this means for you: in Yoruba terms the first thing to look after is your own Orí, through good character and attention to yourself, before asking any god for anything.

21. The three ways destiny is taken

Àkúnlẹ̀yàn is what you knelt and chose in heaven. Àkúnlẹ̀gbà is what you knelt and were given. Àyànmọ́ is what is fixed and comes with you (PhilArchive AKATIA; american-ifa.com).

What this means for you: part of your path is your own choice, part was handed to you, and part is fixed. Ifá treats the first two as open to influence through ẹbọ and character.

22. Àjàlá and the three friends

In the story, Afúwàpẹ́, Orìṣẹ̀ẹ̀kú and Orílẹ̀mẹ̀rẹ̀ go to Àjàlá, the potter who moulds heads. Only Afúwàpẹ́ consults Ifá first and chooses a good Orí. The other two pick heads that look fine but crack (Abimbọ́la 1976).

What this means for you: you were born on Ifá's day. That's the calendar version of Afúwàpẹ́'s choice to consult first.

23. Ìwà (character)

Ìwà pẹ̀lẹ́, gentle character, is called the true partner of Orí. Ifá verses describe Ìwà as Ọ̀rúnmìlà's wife, who leaves him when she is treated badly (Abimbọ́la 1976).

What this means for you: whatever any sign says, character is the part of your destiny you can steer.

24. Ẹlẹ́rìí-ìpín

Ọ̀rúnmìlà is called Ẹlẹ́rìí-ìpín, "witness of the choosing of destiny", because he was present when every Orí was chosen (ipilese.com; Abimbọ́la 1976).

What this means for you: you were born on the witness's own day. In this framework that's the closest thing to having your choice witnessed.

Synthesis of Part IV: Orí teaching puts responsibility on the person. Your calendar items put you in Ifá's domain, and the constructed sign adds lineage and ancestors. Taken together they describe a destiny that grows by consulting, remembering and keeping good character.

Part V — Orisha correspondences

25. The day's orisha: Ọ̀rúnmìlà / Ifá

He works together with Èṣù (who carries the messages) and Ọ̀ṣun (who in many verses stands beside him in divination).

What this means for you: these three are your "day household": wisdom, messages and sweetness.

26. The Odù links

Ọ̀yẹ̀kú is connected to the ancestors and Egúngún, the masquerade of the returning dead. Ìrosùn is connected to ọsùn camwood and remembrance.

What this means for you: ancestors and lineage memory are your sign's two powers.

27. Òdí's orisha in the cowrie system

In cowrie lineages, Òdí's messages are linked to particular òrìṣà houses, and the link varies by lineage (Bascom 1980).

What this means for you: in cowrie terms you'd be asked to take care of hidden things and bring them out in the open.

28. Ògún and the road

Ire ìṣẹ́gun (victory) is linked in practice with Ògún, who clears the road, and with Èṣù, who opens it.

What this means for you: your victory has Ògún's character, which is cutting through.

Synthesis of Part V: Ọ̀rúnmìlà governs your day, the ancestors and Egúngún govern your sign's right leg, memory governs its left leg, and Ògún and Èṣù govern its ire.

Part VI — The Yoruba sky

29. Oòrùn, the Sun

The Sun was 12.7° above the western horizon at azimuth 248°, about an hour before sunset. Its ecliptic longitude was 200°.

What this means for you: you were born at the end of the day, in the evening light. In this tradition that's Ọ̀yẹ̀kú's hour.

30. Àgùàlà (Ajá Òṣùpá), Venus

The Yoruba name for Venus is Àgùàlà, and also Ajá Òṣùpá, "the Moon's dog" (yo.wikipedia "Àgùàlà"). At your birth Venus was only 4.4° from the Sun, close to superior conjunction and lost in the glare.

What this means for you: the evening star was present but hidden. It was there at your birth and unseen. That matches Òdí (the hidden thing) and Ìrosùn (what is remembered rather than seen).

31. Òṣùpá, the Moon

The Moon was 20.5° below the horizon in the northwest.

What this means for you: the Moon wasn't in your birth sky, which makes this a "Sun-and-hidden-star" birth.

32. Other planets

Mercury was 16.9° up in the southwest. Jupiter was rising in the east at −1.0°, right on the horizon. Mars (−6.1°) and Saturn (−19.3°) were below it. I found no reliable Yoruba names for these planets, so they are given their English names.

What this means for you: Jupiter was coming up at the moment you were born. It's the only body crossing the horizon, rising as the Sun goes down.

33. Ìràwọ̀, ìràwọ̀ onírù, Òṣùmàrè

Ìràwọ̀ means star, ìràwọ̀ onírù means comet (the "star with a tail"), and Òṣùmàrè is the rainbow serpent-deity (yoruba-scipedia).

What this means for you: no comet or rainbow is tied to your date. Òṣùmàrè appears in the art below as the bridge between sky and earth.

34. Sirius, the Pleiades and Orion: a gap

Sirius was 62° below the horizon. Some web sources give Yoruba names for these stars, but I couldn't find any reliable attestation for them, so none are given here.

Synthesis of Part VI: the sky backs up the rest of the chapter. A late Sun, a hidden Venus, an absent Moon and a rising Jupiter make a closing-day, hidden-light birth with something rising at the horizon.

Part VII — Fon Fá (Benin)

35. The sixteen Fá du

Gbe, Yeku, Woli, Di, Loso, Wlen, Abla, Akla, Guda, Sa, Ka, Trukpen, Tula, Lete, Ce, Fu. That gives 256 signs (Maupoil, La Géomancie à l'ancienne Côte des Esclaves, 1943).

What this means for you: your constructed sign in Fá is Yeku-Loso.

36. Kpoli, the personal sign

In Fá a person receives their personal sign, the Kpoli, at initiation (Fá-sɛ). It is linked to a person's sɛ (destiny) and is guarded by Legba (Maupoil 1943). It can't be computed.

What this means for you: this is the same kind of gap as the Yoruba itá.

37. Legba

Legba is Fá's messenger, the equivalent of Èṣù.

What this means for you: in both traditions your sign passes through the doorkeeper first.

Synthesis of Part VII: Fá confirms the Yoruba structure. The same sixteen roots and the same 256 signs mean Yeku-Loso is the same reading in a neighbouring language.

Part VIII — Akan day-souls

38. Kra din: Kwabena

An Akan child is first named for the day of birth. A Tuesday-born boy is Kwabena (Kobena, Ebo, Kwabla), and his day-soul is Bena (learnakan.com; UNC Teaching Africa Akan naming lesson). Christaller (1933) explains the kra-din as the soul-name: the kra (soul) takes its destiny (nkrabea) from Onyame on that day.

What this means for you: your Akan name is Kwabena.

39. Bena and the ocean

The UNC lesson links Tuesday with the ocean (Ɛpo / Bosompo).

What this means for you: your soul-day is the ocean's day: depth, a horizon, and things moving underneath. It pairs naturally with Ọ̀yẹ̀kú's night.

40. Kra, sunsum, ntoro, mogya

The kra is the life-soul from Onyame. Sunsum is personality, linked to the father's ntoro. Mogya is the blood of the mother's abusua (clan) (Christaller; Rattray).

What this means for you: your Akan profile puts the ocean-day soul at the centre, with lineage on both sides. Again that matches Ìrosùn.

41. Nkrabea and Orí

Nkrabea is the Akan counterpart of àyànmọ́. Both are a destiny taken before birth.

What this means for you: two neighbouring traditions agree that you came with a destiny already taken.

42. Adaduanan, the 42-day cycle

The Akan ritual calendar runs on a 42-day cycle (6 × 7) anchored to Akwasidae, a Sunday festival. Counting back from the published Akwasidae of 11 October 2026 (Manhyia Palace; nyamedua.org), your birth fell 23 days after an Akwasidae, on day 24 of its cycle. That is between Awukudae and the next Akwasidae.

What this means for you: you were born in an ordinary working stretch, not on a festival. That fits a destiny built by work.

Synthesis of Part VIII: Kwabena, the ocean, and a soul whose destiny was taken from Onyame. The Akan system supports both Orí theory and the sign's lineage theme.

Part IX — Dogon (Mali)

43. Sirius and the Griaule question

Marcel Griaule and Germaine Dieterlen claimed the Dogon knew Sirius was a double star (po tolo / sigu tolo). Walter van Beek's restudy (Current Anthropology 32(2), 1991, 139–167) found that the Dogon call Sirius dana tolo, "the hunter's star", and that no informant knew of Sirius as a double. See also Ortiz de Montellano 1996 and Brecher 1979. The Griaule material is contested and used here only as myth reported by Griaule.

What this means for you: Sirius was far below the horizon at your birth. All this item gives you is a warning against taking dramatic claims at face value.

44. The Sigui

The Sigui is the Dogon renewal ceremony, held about every 60 years. The last one ran from 1967 to 1973. The next is scheduled to begin in 2027 at Youga and to travel through the villages until about 2034 (bamada.net; notrenation.com; Partners Pays-Dogon 2026).

What this means for you: you were born between two Siguis, and the next one starts next year. You're old enough to see the cycle turn.

45. Nommo

The Nommo are the water-spirits of Griaule's account (contested).

What this means for you: water again. With Bena (ocean) and Ọ̀yẹ̀kú (night), this is the third water and depth signal.

Synthesis of Part IX: the Dogon material adds timing. The Sigui renewal begins in 2027.

Part X — Neighbouring systems (noted)

46. Igbo Afa / Áfà, Ewe Afa, Arabic geomancy

The Igbo dibia's Afa and Ewe Afa use the same 16 roots and 256 signs. The 16-figure structure is also shared with Arabic khatt al-raml geomancy and Malagasy sikidy, and scholars still argue about where it started (Maupoil 1943; Bascom 1969).

What this means for you: your constructed sign would carry over into all of these.

Prophecy markers

Part XI — Prophecy: awaited figures, tested marker by marker

Ifá doesn't promise a single messiah. Ọ̀rúnmìlà went back to heaven after his children offended him. He left the sixteen ìkín, telling them to consult those instead of waiting for him to return (Abimbọ́la 1976; ipilese.com cosmology-orunmila). The awaited figures below are tested against your markers. The markers you carry come first.

47. Ọ̀rúnmìlà's witness, the Ifá-chosen one

Markers you carry: - Born on Ọjọ́ Ọ̀rúnmìlà, the day of Ifá. - Constructed Ifá sign with ire ìṣẹ́gun. - Born at day's end with Venus hidden in the Sun: "concealed light". - Odù of memory (Ìrosùn), the theme of carrying the teaching forward.

Markers that can't be shown from these data: ìtẹ̀fá initiation; a real itá confirming the sign; recognition by a babaláwo lineage.

48. Odùduwa / Ọ̀rànmíyàn, the restorer-king

Markers you carry: - An ancestor-first sign, Ọ̀yẹ̀kú, which is the restorer's pattern of renewing by honouring the line. - Ìrosùn: keeping the lineage's name alive. - Ire ìṣẹ́gun: victory over opposition. - Born in Ọ̀wàrà, the storm month.

Markers that can't be shown: royal lineage (idílé ọba), choice by the kingmakers, and the Ifá consultation for an ọba's selection.

49. Okomfo Anokye and the Golden Stool (Akan)

Markers you carry: - Kwabena, the ocean soul-day. - Born in a working stretch of the adaduanan cycle. - A destiny (nkrabea) taken from Onyame.

Markers that can't be shown: priesthood (ɔkɔmfoɔ), a stool lineage.

50. The Nommo's return (Dogon, Griaule, contested)

Markers you carry: - Born between Siguis, with the 2027 renewal ahead in your lifetime. - Water/ocean signals in three systems.

Markers that can't be shown: Dogon initiation, Awa society membership.

Synthesis of Part XI: what you carry is consistent. You were born on Ifá's own day, under a constructed sign of ancestors, memory and victory, with a hidden evening star, an ocean soul-day, and the Sigui renewal arriving within your lifetime. The traditions themselves place the deciding markers in living institutions: initiation, lineage and community recognition.

The 13th-child marker (born the 13th, youngest of 13)

[Tradition] In the Ifá order of the sixteen principal Odù, Òtúrá is the thirteenth. In Akan naming he is Kwabena, the Tuesday-born.

Interpretation What he carries. The thirteenth Odù, Òtúrá Méjì, is the sign of peace and of spiritual vision, a sign associated with prayer. As the thirteenth child, born on the thirteenth, he stands under the thirteenth Odù's number.

Integrated reading

You were born on Ifá's own day, in the storm month, at dusk, while the evening star was hidden in the Sun and Jupiter was rising in the east. Your constructed Ifá sign is Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Ìrosùn (Fá: Yeku-Loso). That means night and the ancestors, working through red camwood and memory. Ìbò gives ire ìṣẹ́gun, victory over opposition. The cowries add Òdí, the hidden thing brought out, and the kola says yes, confirm it. The Akan call you Kwabena, a soul of Tuesday and the ocean. The Dogon would place you between two Sigui renewals, with the next starting in 2027.

Every one of these systems keeps coming back to depth, inheritance and coming through. Orí teaching says your character (ìwà) is the part you steer. Consult before choosing, as Afúwàpẹ́ did. Honour the line, as Ọ̀yẹ̀kú asks. Keep its memory alive, as Ìrosùn asks. Then the victory promised by ire ìṣẹ́gun comes in the second movement, after the confirmation the kola asks for.

What this means for you, in one line: your West African profile is that of a keeper of memory who wins through resistance. Under the Kọ́jọ́dá convention your birth sits inside Ifá's own day. Your real Odù is still waiting for a babaláwo.
